Marvel’s Spider-Man Draft Guide

Tom Anderson October 2, 2025Events, Limited

Welcome readers, to the official Card Kingdom guide to Marvel’s Spider-Man Draft! Also known as Through the Omenpaths, Part 1 Draft for anyone on MTG Arena! 

This is a unique and weird set design with equally unique and weird changes to its default draft rules. If you want a rundown of the Pick-Two format and how it might affect your usual Draft strategy, then check out the full preview of those rules I wrote a couple weeks back. 

I won’t go over the same points again for this guide, but I will say that I did all the research on this format playing Pick-Two, so the earlier discussion is certainly applicable. Instead, we’re now going to take a more detailed look into the gameplay and archetypes of Marvel’s Spider-Man.

SHAPE OF THE FORMAT

Being such a small set makes this interesting to analyse, as each individual card has much more influence on gameplay. The commons in particular will be showing up in multiples every draft, and if you know which ones can form the backbone of your deck you can replicate very similar-looking versions of your favorite archetype draft after draft.

The pace of play in this set is relatively slow and defined entirely by how your creatures’ statlines trade off with the opponent. Removal in Spider-Man is considerably below-average power, and even alternative ways to combat big creatures like deathtouch or combat tricks are thin on the ground. 

This elevates both huge threats like Rhino, Barreling Brute and the few reasonable answers, like The Spot’s Portal. It also makes it much harder to run people over with tempo, unless you curve out with strong rares or can manage to double-spell using mayhem multiple times in the first few turns.

If we assume both players will usually stabilize into some kind of board stall, we should prioritize picking up value engines, mana sinks, and evasive attackers as ways to eventually break that parity. Yes, somehow flying attackers are still very powerful even in the set full of reach-having spiders. Try it if you don’t believe me.

When it comes to defining identity and strategy for your draft deck, Spider-Man offers up a handful of very broad, simple themes. As a result, almost every card in a given color pair has some benefit to offer that archetype’s gameplan. You’ll even find yourself stumbling across good synergy with many out-of-color cards!

That cross-color mechanical overlap means you can quite easily play an enemy color pair instead of the “officially supported” ones, and it seems like most draft decks actually end up as “2.5 color” where you’re splashing two-to-four powerful or synergistic spells from outside your color pair.

The Villain deck is a great example of this. Yes, technically the official breakdown lists there being a UB connive deck and a RB mayhem deck. But those two mechanics complement each other so naturally that it feels silly not to pair them. When you realize that all three colors are further connected through strong typal support for villains, it makes more sense to refer to the “UBR Villain Discard” archetype than either half individually.

COLOR THEORY

The unique nature of this set means that not all color pairs are officially given “archetype” status with two-color uncommon enablers to define their strategy. That doesn’t mean you can’t draft a different pair like white-black, but doing so successfully relies more on opening specific rares than anything replicable I could teach in this guide. Even when you first-pick a card like Mister Negative or The Spot, Living Portal, you should probably be thinking of how to splash it into a UW or UB deck rather than trying to force pure WB – it’s just too improbable. 

On the subject of splashing third and fourth colors, I should mention that fixing has been one of the areas where it seems Take Two rules influences drafting phase strategy the most!  

Spider-Man has at least an average amount of fixing if you just look at the spoiler. There’s a cycle of two-color lands plus a filter-land and Evolving Wilds, with at least one of those options appearing each pack. We have the familiar fixing loadout of a Manalith and a Campus Guide, along with a handful of green mana creatures. Other color combinations at least benefit from the hybrid mana costs which appear across the set, reducing the rate of unplayable draws.

This should be more than enough fixing cards for a small set, but in practice it can be surprisingly hard to get your hands on them, especially the lands. My theory is that the Pick 2 rules are making it far easier for players to take these lands early, even first-picking them in a lot of cases. Where in a classic draft it’s often a tough call whether to take an early fixing land or the best spell in your color, in Take 2 you just grab both!

I’d recommend you consider that approach yourself, especially since there’s only a couple of commons and uncommons in this set which really stand out as potential gamechanger picks.

The other approach you can take with Pick 2 drafting is to decisively cut or force a single color essentially from the start of the draft! If the average pack has two decent picks in each color, just hard committing to take those two cards from every pack you see will very quickly send a message to the other three players, and leave you with a strong base color to build on in packs two and three.

This probably means you aren’t taking the best cards available every time, and it can be punishing if another player also opens their draft with the same strategy. But it does seem to deliver consistent results –  which makes Spider-Man a really approachable experience for newer and more casual drafters who tend to “lock in” on a color by default.

ARCHETYPE BREAKDOWN

I’ve decided to list the decks as I feel they exist in practice, rather than stick to the prescribed two-color pairs. That said, if you are determined to play a pure WU or WG deck rather than combine the two, most of the advice I give for the three-color deck will still apply – the colors are just that similar in their identity.

WUG: MODIFIED MIDRANGE

These colors are all focused on buffing up one or more large creatures so they can safely attack into opposing blockers. White-blue has the specific payoffs for modified creatures, but the best enablers for those are often the +1/+1 counter cards in white and white-green. Web-Shooters is the only really exciting way to modify creatures besides counters, and you’ll happily play it here, but it’s far easier to aim for stable, steady growth through counters. 

Use Wild Pack Squad to enable “free” attacks with your biggest body to pressure the opponent, and leave everything else at home for an impenetrable defense. Eventually you’ll find a way to crack that status quo open further: attacking unblockably with Wraith, Vicious Vigilante, drawing cards with SP//dr plus flyers, or just chaining back to back copies of Web-Warriors. 

Save your removal to snipe anything too big for you to safely block, or to clear away reach and flying blockers if you’re taking that path to victory. The exception is probably Sudden Strike: as a rare piece of low-cost instant speed removal, that card is best used as a combat trick to heavily punish any attempted multiblock on your big, vigilant, first-striking attacker.

URB:  VILLAIN DISCARD CONTROL

Probably the coolest and most strongly-defined archetype in the set, these colors combine multiple mechanics centered around discarding cards. The mayhem mechanic lets you cast a card you’ve discarded as though it was still in hand, often at a reduced cost. Since cards like Pumpkin Bombardment use discarding as a drawback, you can effectively negate that downside while casting a second cheaper-than-usual spell in the same turn!

While it’s not guaranteed to work out that neatly every time, there’s enough density of discard effects to make mayhem (and the other payoffs like Green Goblin, Revenant) attractive. Conniving creatures such as Prowler or Dock Ock’s Henchmen are the best enablers of all, adding the value of +1/+1 counters on top of sculpting your hand and setting up mayhem plays. 

Ideally, you can swamp your opponent in the opening turns with insanely efficient combos like Pumpkin Bombardment + Raging Goblinoids or Scarlet Spider, Kaine + Swarm, Being of Bees. But the villain typal support (and accompanying black removal package) give you options to play a more controlling build that grinds games out or goes for sudden lethal off Vulture, Scheming Scavenger.

RGx: BIG SPELL RAMP

The RG theme is classic ramp with specific payoffs for casting spells with mana value four or more – an excellent fit for a set like this with mediocre removal and a sluggish pace of play. Once you’ve assembled enough mana to cast these chunky spells, value engines in Rhino, Barreling Brute and Superior Foes of Spider-Man make sure you can chain them together and bury your opponents under a mountain of power and toughness! 

The obvious challenge here is making sure the deck still curves out well and competes efficiently at all stages of the game. Mana dorks like Spider Manifestation and Guy in the Chair help you accelerate your gameplan and give opponents less time to pressure you before the big bodies arrive. The latter also provides fixing, which is important for a deck so eager to splash bombs from across the color pie. With enough fixing cards you can make this an outright three color deck (I like URG), or make red the splash color instead if your red cards go missing in packs 2 and 3.

Something to look out for when drafting RGx are cards which double as both early and late-game plays. Cheap bodies like Angry Rabble, Spiders-Man and Lurking Lizards help contest the board early while continuing to scale as your mana pool expands in future turns. On the other end of the spectrum, Stegron the Dinosaur Man can give you an extra “free” slot for a four-plus mana spell, but is never a dead draw thanks to its two-cost combat trick mode.

WRG+: SPIDER TYPAL

For a product named after and heavily featuring Spider-Man, there’s not quite as much emphasis on spider typal in this set at one might expect. But the options are there, and there are specifically a lot of rares which can make building around spiders rewarding for you. If you’ve just opened up a draft by first-picking Cosmic Spider-Man, or even just the opening double of Aunt May + Wall Crawl, I’m glad to say that Rainbow Spiders is a lot more real and consistent than your average five-color Limited deck.

Of course, you don’t actually need to always force yourself into four or five colors to build around these synergies. The core of this deck – spider creatures and buffs – is solidly in green, white and red. In many ways you could look at this archetype as an extension of the UGW deck, because team-wide buffs like Wall Crawl and Web-Warriors incentivize the same steady build-up of your army. 

The difference is in how many copies of the spider-specific support cards you grab early, or how many splashable rare spiders you can pick up. The more of those cards you get, the more you can bias your draft towards taking specifically spider creatures instead of just WUG. Make sure to also grab a few extra fixing cards: Guy in the Chair is flexible and at least has spider synergy, but Spider-Man, Brooklyn Visionary is even better. Scout the City and Pictures of Spider-Man are sneaky second options which can help smooth your draws and have utility besides.

BRG: ALL-IN TEMPO

This is arguably the most experimental and non-obvious deck I’ll put in the guide, somehow moreso than the five-color typal pile! Playing RGx aggro is not an option the designers have advertised for this set, and it actively goes against the primary theme of RG – expensive spells. 

But hear me out: Marvel’s Spider-Man does actually have a number of very strong, very cheap instants to complement aggressive creature decks. There’s also extremely high potential efficiency out of the RB mayhem package, with relatively common opportunities to cast creatures at a 1-2 mana discount. A lot of these cards are underpicked, precisely because they do not gel well with the strategic direction of most decks. The implication is that you can make a decision to pick up all the loose copies of Grow Extra Arms and Hobgoblin, Mantled Maruader and use them to run people over.

Most of the core effects we need are in RG, which gives you the flexibility to play RGw or RGb depending on what’s open. That core consists of cheap evasive attackers (Angry Rabble, Hobgoblin, Scarlet Spider), plus removal (Rhino’s Rampage, Shock, Pumpkin Bombardment) and pump spells (Grow Extra Arms, Heroes’ Hangout, Stegron). If you’re in black, just expand the mayhem package as much as you can and perhaps add removal. If you’re in white, you can create that same tempo with discounted web-slinging creatures, then establish combat superiority with Wild Pack Squad, Sudden Strike and Thwip!.

BITTEN BY A RADIOACTIVE BOOSTER PACK

I really wasn’t sure what to expect coming into Marvel’s Spider-Man Limited. I’ve been primarily a Draft player for a solid decade now, and in that time Magic has changed in ebbs and flows, but nothing like what it feels we’re going through now. 

Innovations like small-set Universes Beyond products being Standard legal AND draftable were not on my radar even a year ago. But it is very interesting to see how those changes force the set design to adapt, and what it reinforces to me is how rock-solid the core design of this game really is. I’m not ready to advocate for Pick-Two draft rules to become the norm in every set, but I found them to add some unique spice and strategy to this format, and the benefits for newer players or smaller groups who want to draft are obvious. 

For fellow players who were (like me) on the fence, I hope this draft guide excites you enough to hop off the fence and give the set some play time. Spider-Man is a cool little bubble of Limited play with enough sizzle to at least tide us all over until the next bright new thing.
